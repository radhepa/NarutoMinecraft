# Small talk, part A: the village as a whole. Places, prices, customs, rules, what's coming up.

## chat.village  fn=worldbuild
You can see the Hokage Rock from every street in the Leaf. People say that's on purpose: nobody's ever out of sight of the faces.
Four faces on the Rock, and plenty of bare stone beside them. The carvers say they left room on purpose.
Every spring the stonemasons climb the Rock to clear the moss from the faces. They hang off ropes like spiders. Brave folk.
When the morning sun hits the Hokage Rock, the faces go gold for about a minute. Early risers stop in the street to watch.
Up on top of the Rock it's all forest. Kids say you can see the whole village from there like a map. They're right.
The Hokage Residence is the big round building right under the faces. Fire painted on the roof. You can't miss it.
Mission Desk on the ground floor, the Hokage's office at the top. Everything in between the Residence holds is paperwork.
Walking the whole wall round the Leaf takes half a morning. Guards walk it every night.
Two characters are painted on the main gate's doors, one on each. Every child learns what they mean in their first week.
Even the Hokage signs the gate guards' ledger. Everyone who comes and goes goes in that book.
Travellers coming in through the gate always stop and stare at the Rock first. You can tell the newcomers that way.
The Academy sits at the foot of the Rock. Big fire sign out front, and that old swing hanging from the tree.
That swing in front of the Academy has been there longer than most teachers. Generations of children have sat on it.
Inside the Academy there's a library, a workshop and a yard full of target posts. The posts are older than they look and very scarred.
On graduation days the Academy yard is full of parents. Spring and autumn, every year. The whole street smells of celebration rice.
Ichiraku's been serving ramen since before I was born. A few stools at a counter, and the best broth in the Leaf.
Ichiraku's counter seats only a handful. Everyone ends up elbow to elbow, Genin, Jonin and grandmothers alike.
Right through the middle of the Leaf runs the river. Most bridges are wood. The old stone one near the market is the oldest.
In summer the children jump off the low bridge into the deep pool. The adults pretend not to notice. Some join in.
Fishermen line the river at dawn. You can buy their catch right off the bank, if you're quick.
From the main street to the river, the market street sells fish, vegetables, tea, weapons. Everything on one long walk.
Market Day comes once a week. Farmers bring their carts in through the gate at dawn. It's the busiest morning of the week.
The hospital's the tall white building near the herb gardens. Medics and nurses work day and night there.
If you collapse or get hurt on a mission, you'll wake up in the hospital. The beds are hard but the nurses are kind.
Mint and something bitter: that's the smell of the Suzuran drying racks in the gardens by the hospital.
The shrine's up the long stone steps. People go there at New Year, for weddings, and when they need to think.
At dawn and dusk the shrine bell rings. If you hear it, you know the time without looking at the sun.
Twenty-five clans live in the Leaf, from the great compounds to the small trade families. Most folk belong to none.
The Senju compound is beside the Residence. Their hall is grown around a living tree. Leaves come through the roof in spring.
Next to the Senju stands the Uzumaki memorial shrine. The spiral crest is carved on every lantern there.
Close to the centre is the Hyuga estate. Two halves, a dojo and a koi pond people say is older than the wall.
Out at the edge of the village, by their deer forest, live the Nara. The deer wander out sometimes and eat people's flowers.
The Akimichi compound is by the market street. Their kitchen hall smokes from dawn till night. The barbecue house is theirs too.
Every wedding and every funeral in the Leaf has flowers from the Yamanaka shop on the main street.
You'll hear the Inuzuka ninken before you see them. Their kennels and the vet clinic are right by the main gate.
The Uchiha compound is on the outskirts, walled, with their fan crest on every wall. The police headquarters is theirs.
At the wooded edge of a residential street sits the Aburame clan home. Their garden hums. Not unpleasantly.
Near the Residence there's the Sarutobi house, with a monkey crest over the door and a yard for fire training.
On the old artists' lane live the Kurama. Their walls are painted inside and out. Some of the paintings look back at you.
The Gekko keep a sword dojo by the training grounds with a platform for watching the moon. Very quiet place.
Minor clans live among everyone else. The Hagane forge on the market street, the Kinoshita in the carpenters' lane.
Half the teachers in the Leaf are related to the other half. Most are Umino, living in the streets near the Academy.
Every sensei has a story about Ground 3. Three stumps in a row, a river nearby. The training grounds are all numbered.
Outside the wall there's a fenced forest with forty-four gates. Training Ground 44. The trees there are as wide as houses.
When the stadium's full for the Chunin Exam finals, you can hear the crowd from the market.
Nearly every big street has a bathhouse. The oldest has a painted mountain on the wall behind the water.
Six generations of one family have run the tea house by the bridge. The cups are older than the furniture.
On festival nights the barbecue house is full to the door. You order by the plate, and they never stop coming.
Every season the flower shop changes its window. People walk past just to see what's in it.
Blank scrolls, ink, sealing paper: the scroll shop's owner can tell how many sheets you need just by looking at you.
People go into the furniture shop just to sit on the chairs. Every piece they make is in the showroom.
Out back of the weapon shop is the forge. You can hear the hammers from the market street all day.
Onigiri wrapped and ready by the grocer's door every morning. Shinobi grab them on the way out.
Old armour, odd swords, other villages' coins: the pawn shop on the corner takes anything. Pays half, never more.
Pink, white and green. People argue about the right order to eat the dango from the shop by the river.
Round water tanks sit on the Leaf's roofs. On hot days, kids climb up and dip their feet in them. Not allowed. Very common.
Most of the Leaf's homes are plaster, in cream and terracotta. When the sun's low, the whole village looks warm.
Lanterns hang along the main street. The lamp keepers light them at dusk and put them out at dawn.
You can follow your nose from the main gate to the carpenters' lane. Fresh cedar, always.
The Uchiha police walk the main street at night. They're strict, but they'll walk you home if you're lost.
Shinobi apartment blocks are stacked and plain. Nobody needs fancy rooms when half the tenants are never home.
Our water comes from springs up behind the Rock. That's why it's so cold and sweet.
There's a memorial stone at one of the training grounds. People leave flowers and stand there quietly. You'll see it.
All the main streets point at the faces, like spokes of a wheel. The founders built the Leaf in a fan around the Rock.

Shinobi in the Leaf travel by rooftop as often as by street. The tiles on the market street get replaced every year because of it.
Every shinobi in the Leaf wears the headband somewhere. Forehead, arm, belt. The way you wear it says something about you.

Village rule: no fighting in the streets. Disputes go to the training grounds or the police. That's been the law since the founding.
Jutsu aren't allowed in the market. A fire jutsu near paper lanterns? You can imagine the trouble.
Roof-running is allowed, but not on the shrine or the Residence. People who forget get a visit from the police.
In the Leaf, you take your sandals off before stepping onto someone's tatami. Even in a hurry. Even on a mission.
It's custom to bow to the Hokage Rock when you come back from far away. Some do it, some just look up and smile.
You don't whistle at night near the shrine. Old superstition. Most people still won't do it.
When someone new moves onto a street, the neighbours bring rice. It's how you say welcome in the Leaf.
When a squad leaves on a long mission, families tie a strip of cloth to the gate post. It comes down when they're home.
In the Leaf you never pour your own tea when someone else is at the table. You pour theirs, and they pour yours.
No curfew for grown folk here, but the shops close by evening and the streets get quiet fast.
Everyone stops working when the shrine bell rings at New Year. Even the forge goes silent.
Clans of every kind live side by side here. The Inuzuka's dogs sleep on the Hyuga steps. Nobody minds. Mostly.
In the Leaf, the Hokage's name is on every mission scroll. It's a reminder that each job is for the whole village.
Farmers, traders, orphans: the Leaf takes in people from everywhere. If you live here, you're Leaf.
There's an old saying here: "the leaves grow from one tree". It means we all share the same roots.

## chat.village  fn=worldbuild,commerce
A bowl of ramen at Ichiraku runs about eight ryo. More if you want extra pork. Worth every coin.
Onigiri from the grocer cost three ryo. A cup of tea's about five. Cheapest meal in the village.
Kunai at the weapon shop run five to forty ryo, depending on the steel. Shuriken are cheaper. Smoke bombs are a fortune.
Soldier pills cost sixty ryo each. Most shinobi carry one and pray they never need it.
Furniture's dear. A good table costs more than a month of ramen. People save for years for a proper chest of drawers.
Festival games cost a ryo or two. The prizes are mostly paper fans and bags of sweets. People play anyway.
A new outfit from the tailor costs a few hundred ryo. Most people wear the same three until they fall apart.
Dango's two ryo a skewer at the stand by the river. Three if you want the sweet sauce.
Prices go up before festivals. The grocer says it's demand. Everyone else says it's the grocer.
Two ryo for the bathhouse, or one if you bring your own towel. Children go free with a parent.
Rice is cheapest in autumn after the harvest. In late winter, everybody's counting grains.
A part-time shift at Ichiraku or the grocer pays forty to eighty ryo. Not bad for a few hours of chopping onions.
A single stem at the flower shop is a ryo, a whole bouquet ten. Lots of people buy one stem every week.
Steel for a smith costs a few ryo a bar. Good steel costs more. Cheap steel costs more in the end.

## chat.village  fn=guide
The Mission Desk is on the Residence's ground floor. Eight in the morning till six in the evening.
Ichiraku's on the main street. Follow your nose. If you smell pork broth, you're there.
Near the herb gardens, white walls, big doors: that's the hospital. You'll see medics going in and out.
Up the long stone steps at the end of the lane is the shrine. Count the steps if you like. Nobody agrees on the number.
Want to see the whole village? Climb the stairs to the top of the Hokage Rock. The view's worth the legs.
Off the main street, running down to the river, is the market street. You can buy nearly anything there.
Anyone can walk round the furniture shop's showroom. You can see every piece they sell before you buy.
Want a ninken pup? Ask at the Inuzuka clinic by the main gate.
Anyone can read in the Academy library, but keep quiet. The librarian is very fast.
Look for the smoke and listen for the hammers. That's the weapon shop and forge, on the market street.
Blue curtains, by the bridge: the tea house. Best place to sit and watch the river.
Next to the Akimichi compound is the barbecue house. Go hungry. Leave happy.
On the corner of the market street is the pawn shop. Bring things you don't need. Leave with ryo.
Training grounds are past the edge of the houses. Ground 3 is the one with the three stumps.
Sealing paper? Try the scroll shop on the market street, near the forge.
Look for the red umbrella by the second bridge. That's the dango stand.
The flower shop is on the main street, at the front of the Yamanaka compound. Can't miss the colours.
For hot water and a lot of chatter, go to the oldest bathhouse, the one with the mountain painting.
At the end of the main street is the main gate. That's where the squads leave and the travellers come in.
Look for the swing in front and the big fire sign. That's the Academy, at the foot of the Rock.

Every festival has a committee of shopkeepers who argue for a month and then make it perfect in one night.
The shrine keeps a great drum that's only played at New Year and weddings. You feel it in your chest from the market.
Visiting teams for the Chunin Exams lodge at the inns near the gate. The innkeepers learn a dozen new accents a season.
New Genin always buy their first proper kunai pouch the week after graduation. The weapon shop does a roaring trade.
On festival nights the bridges are closed to carts. Too many people, too many lanterns, and far too much dancing.
Festival stall spots are handed out by the market council. Families keep the same spot for generations and guard it fiercely.
The fireworks for every festival are made by one family outside the wall. Nobody's allowed to watch them work.
Each clan has its own clan day. The Akimichi feast is the one everyone hopes to be invited to.
Weddings at the shrine are open to the whole street. You don't need to know the couple to throw rice.
Market Day turns into a kind of fair when the weather's good. Music, jugglers, and the fishmongers' shouting contest.

Half the steps in the Leaf were cut by the Tsuchigumo families. They live in stone houses by the quarry.
The Shiranui house near the gate barracks has a wall bristling with senbon. Target practice, decades of it.
Near the training grounds the Namiashi run a little footwork dojo. You can hear the tapping from the street, like rain.
The Haruno family's garden on the residential street is the neatest in the Leaf. Not a weed dares.
In a grove of old trees sits the Yuhi house. People say the paths there are never quite where you left them.
On a quiet lane is the Hatake house, a lone old place with a small yard. Most people walk past without noticing it.
The Tobari live behind the wall in shuttered houses. Their lane is the quietest place in the village. Even footsteps go soft.
Barred windows on the Morino house near the police headquarters. The family's perfectly polite. The house just looks grim.
Look under your table. Most of the furniture in the Leaf was made in the Kinoshita yard, and their mark's there.
Every hinge in the Leaf still swings thanks to the Hagane metalworkers. Their shop's beside the forge.
On harvest day the Akimichi feed anyone who helps carry the rice sacks into their storehouse. The line is very long.
Feed the Nara deer if you like, but you can't chase them. They're protected. They know it, too. Bold creatures.
The Inuzuka ninken have the run of the gate district. If a big dog sits in your path, you wait. That's just manners.
For a ryo, the ferryman at the wide bend takes you across. There's a bridge further down, but the ferry's more fun.
Each district has a well with a little roof over it. People meet at the wells in the morning. That's where news starts.
Letters to the farms and the border villages go from the courier office near the Residence. Couriers run the roads every day.
The Leaf's oldest residents sit on the benches by the main street every afternoon and rate everyone's walk. Be warned.
At night the lanterns on the wall's watchtowers look like a ring of stars around the village.
There's a sound the Leaf makes at dusk: shutters closing, pots on stoves, dogs being called home. You'll learn it.
No two bridges over the river are quite alike. The Kinoshita built the newest, the founders built the oldest.
Each stone lantern along the shrine path was donated by a family. Their crests are carved on the bases.
The Leaf has gardens tucked everywhere: on roofs, in alleys, on balconies. If there's sun, somebody's growing something.
Most houses in the Leaf keep a lantern by the door. It's lit when someone's out late, so they can find their way home.
Before dawn the bakery on the main street opens. The smell reaches the gate. The night guards call it their alarm.
Behind the market street there's a maze of alleys with tiny workshops: lacquer, paper, combs, kites. Worth getting lost in.
For hard winters there's a village storehouse, run by the Akimichi and the council. Rice for everyone, if the snow comes.
Every year on the founding day, the oldest residents tell stories in the square. The stories get bigger every year.
There's a little island in the river with one pine and a great many ducks. Children swim out to it every summer.
Herons stand by the fishmongers' stalls by the river like customers. The fishmongers throw them scraps.
